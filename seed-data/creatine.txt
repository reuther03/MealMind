Creatine
Creatine monohydrate is the most effective and best-studied legal supplement for increasing high-intensity exercise capacity and lean mass; this guide covers how it works, how to dose it, who responds, water retention, safety and common myths.

How creatine works
About 95% of the body's creatine is stored in skeletal muscle, with small amounts in the brain and testes. Roughly two thirds is stored as phosphocreatine (PCr). During short, intense efforts such as sprints and heavy sets, PCr quickly donates a phosphate to regenerate ATP through the creatine kinase reaction. More stored PCr means more repetitions, more power and more total training volume, which over weeks leads to greater gains in strength and muscle. Creatine is made in the liver and kidneys from arginine and glycine, and the body needs to replace about 1-3 g per day.

Muscle creatine stores
A normal diet with 1-2 g/day of creatine (from meat and fish) leaves muscle stores about 60-80% saturated. The average total creatine pool is about 120 mmol per kg of dry muscle, with an upper limit near 160 mmol/kg. Supplementation raises muscle creatine and PCr by about 20-40%. One pound (about 450 g) of raw beef or salmon provides only about 1-2 g, so reaching full stores from food alone is impractical.

Loading protocol
- The fastest way to saturate muscle is a loading phase of about 0.3 g per kg of body weight per day for 5-7 days (about 20-25 g/day, or 21 g for a 70 kg person), split into 4-5 servings of about 5 g.
- After loading, take a maintenance dose of 3-5 g per day.

Without loading
Loading is not required. Taking 3-5 g per day (or about 0.1 g/kg) from the start fills muscle stores over about 3-4 weeks. The end result is the same; loading only gets there faster, which is useful before a competition.

Timing and how to take it
Consistency matters more than timing. Studies comparing 5 g taken before versus after training found no meaningful difference. Taking creatine with carbohydrate, or carbohydrate plus protein, increases muscle uptake, although the performance benefit may not be larger than creatine alone. Creatine powder is very stable for years, but it slowly breaks down to creatinine in liquid, especially in warm, acidic drinks, so mix it shortly before drinking rather than storing pre-mixed drinks.

Responders and non-responders
Not everyone gains the same amount. People whose muscle stores are already high, for example those with high meat intake, have less room to increase and respond less. Vegetarians have lower stores (about 90-110 mmol/kg dry muscle) and often see larger increases. Benefits are clearest for high-intensity, repeated efforts lasting seconds to a few minutes, and creatine also supports recovery and glycogen storage.

Water retention and weight gain
The most consistent side effect is a gain in body weight. Loading can cause short-term fluid retention of about 0.5-1.0 L, mostly inside muscle cells, which explains much of the quick scale increase some people see in the first week. Longer training studies of 4-10 weeks generally show no lasting change in total body water relative to muscle mass. Creatine does not increase fat mass.

Safety
- Short and long-term use, up to 30 g per day for 5 years, has shown no harmful effects in healthy people. Clinical populations have used 0.3-0.8 g/kg/day for years without serious adverse events.
- Creatine is not an anabolic steroid; it has a different chemical structure and mechanism.
- It does not cause dehydration, muscle cramps or more injuries; athletes using it reported fewer cramps and heat illnesses.
- Creatine appears safe for adolescents in supervised, competitive training when recommended doses are respected.

Kidney myth
Creatine raises blood creatinine, a marker labs use to estimate kidney function, which can make results look worse without any real kidney damage. Over 20 years of controlled research shows no kidney harm at recommended doses in healthy people. The original concern came from a 1998 case report of a man who already had kidney disease and was on medication. People with existing kidney disease should consult a doctor.

Hair loss myth
The hair loss claim comes from one 2009 study in rugby players in which DHT rose by 56% after loading (25 g/day for 7 days) and stayed 40% above baseline after maintenance, but values remained within normal limits. No study has measured hair loss directly, and 12 other studies found no meaningful change in testosterone. Current evidence does not show that creatine causes baldness.

Forms of creatine
Creatine monohydrate is the most researched and effective form and contains about 87.9% creatine by weight. Creatine ethyl ester, buffered creatine, creatine nitrate, creatine salts and creatine dipeptides have not been shown to raise muscle creatine more than monohydrate, and several perform worse. Choose plain micronized or standard creatine monohydrate, preferably third-party tested.
