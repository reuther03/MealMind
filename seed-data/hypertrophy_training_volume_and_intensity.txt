Hypertrophy Training: Volume and Intensity
Evidence-based guidelines for building muscle: how many weekly sets per muscle, which rep ranges and loads work, how close to failure to train, how long to rest and how often to train each muscle.

Weekly volume per muscle group
- Volume, counted as hard sets per muscle per week, is one of the strongest drivers of muscle growth, with a graded dose-response relationship.
- Low volumes of 4 or fewer weekly sets per muscle still produce meaningful growth, which is useful when time is limited.
- At least 10 weekly sets per muscle group are recommended to maximize hypertrophy, usually as 2-3 sets per exercise across several exercises.
- Reviews disagree on whether going well beyond 10 sets adds more: one umbrella review saw no clear extra benefit, while dose-response analyses suggest more volume can add gains for some trained lifters at a higher recovery cost. Increase sets gradually and watch recovery.
- Multiple sets per exercise produced about 40% more hypertrophy than single sets in comparisons of trained and untrained people.

Rep ranges and loading (% 1RM)
- Common categories: high load is above 70% of 1RM (fewer than about 11 reps), moderate is 30-70% (11-20 reps) and low is below 30% (more than 20 reps).
- The traditional "hypertrophy zone" is 8-12 reps, and ACSM recommends emphasizing 6-12 RM loads for hypertrophy within a periodized 1-12 RM range.
- Meta-analyses show similar whole-muscle growth with loads anywhere from about 30% of 1RM up to heavy loads, as long as sets are taken to or close to failure. Below about 30% 1RM growth appears compromised.
- Moderate loads are usually the most time-efficient choice. Light loads (20-30 reps) require many more reps and cause more burning discomfort, while very heavy loads need more sets to match growth and add joint stress.
- In a volume-matched trial, 7 sets of 3RM built the same biceps size as 3 sets of 10RM but caused joint complaints and signs of overreaching.
- Mixing rep ranges across the week is a sensible way to cover strength and size.

Proximity to failure and RIR
- Training effort is critical: sets should end at or near momentary muscular failure, especially with light loads.
- Studies where light-load sets stopped far short of failure showed less hypertrophy than heavier training, while light-load sets taken to failure matched heavy loads.
- Effort can be tracked with repetitions in reserve (RIR), the number of reps you could still do at the end of a set. For hypertrophy, most working sets should finish with very few reps left in reserve.
- Lifters estimate RIR more accurately as they get closer to failure and with more training experience, so beginners tend to underestimate how many reps remain.

Rest intervals between sets
- ACSM guidelines suggest 1-2 minutes of rest between hypertrophy sets at moderate loads.
- Research in trained lifters suggests very short rest (around 1 minute) can blunt growth, and studies often use about 2-3 minutes, especially for big compound lifts.
- Practical rule: rest long enough to repeat a similar number of quality reps on the next set.

Training frequency per muscle
- When weekly volume is equal, training a muscle once or several times per week produces similar growth.
- Higher frequencies help mainly because they make it easier to accumulate more total weekly sets with good quality, so frequency is best used as a tool to distribute volume.
- Splitting 10 or more weekly sets for a muscle across 2 or more sessions keeps per-session fatigue manageable.

Other variables
- Repetition duration between about 0.5 and 8 seconds works similarly; very slow reps of 10 seconds or more are inferior.
- Including the eccentric (lowering) phase matters; eccentric actions may offer extra benefit.
- Exercise order and time of day have no clear effect on growth; choose based on preference and priorities.
- Progressive overload over time, adding load, reps or sets, is required whichever variables you choose.
