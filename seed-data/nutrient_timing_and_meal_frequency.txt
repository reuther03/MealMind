Nutrient Timing and Meal Frequency
When to eat around workouts, how much the "anabolic window" really matters, how many meals per day make sense, what intermittent fasting and time-restricted eating do, and whether protein before sleep helps.

What nutrient timing means
Nutrient timing is planning when food, fortified foods and supplements are eaten relative to training. Good timing can support recovery, tissue repair, muscle protein synthesis (MPS) and mood after hard or high-volume sessions. However, total daily intake of energy and protein matters more than exact timing for most people.

Pre-workout meal
- A larger mixed meal is usually eaten 1-4 hours before training, giving time for digestion.
- Close to the session, choose foods low in fat, fiber and very high protein amounts, because these slow digestion and can cause stomach discomfort during exercise.
- Within the last hour, a small, easily digested snack is optional and depends on personal tolerance.
- Some athletes avoid eating carbohydrate less than 60 minutes before exercise for fear of rebound low blood sugar. Reviews found that feeding at least 60 minutes before exercise did not harm performance and often improved it, so individual tolerance should decide.
- Practise the pre-workout routine in training before using it in competition.

Post-workout meal
- Eating high-quality protein anywhere from immediately to 2 hours after exercise produces a robust increase in MPS.
- A review of off-season bodybuilding suggests a protein-containing meal within 1-2 hours before and within 1-2 hours after training gives a small extra benefit.
- The size and timing of the pre-workout meal changes how urgent the post-workout meal is. If a good meal was eaten 1-2 hours before training, amino acids are still circulating afterwards.

The anabolic window
The old idea that protein must be consumed within 30-60 minutes after training or the session is "wasted" is not supported. The anabolic effect of resistance exercise lasts at least 24 hours, although it slowly decreases with time. Pre- or post-workout protein both work, so the best choice is a matter of individual preference and schedule. Training fasted, for example early in the morning, is the main situation where eating soon after the session matters more.

Protein distribution across the day
Meeting the daily protein target is the priority. After that, spreading protein evenly over the day, with a feeding roughly every 3-4 hours, appears better for MPS than eating most protein in one or two large meals.

Meal frequency
- In people who are not exercising, changing the number of meals per day has little effect on weight loss or body composition when total calories are equal.
- A meta-analysis that initially suggested a benefit of more frequent meals found that the effect was driven by a single study.
- Meal frequency mainly affects appetite and satiety, and results differ between people: some studies found lower hunger with fewer, larger meals, while one found six high-protein meals better than three.
- For practical purposes, 3-6 meals per day all work; choose the pattern that makes it easiest to hit calorie and protein targets.

Intermittent fasting and time-restricted eating
Time-restricted eating (TRE) limits food to a daily window, for example 16 hours of fasting and an 8-hour eating window (16:8). In an 8-week study of 34 resistance-trained men eating the same calories, the group eating three meals between 1 p.m. and 8 p.m. lost more fat mass (about 16% versus about 3% of fat mass) while keeping fat-free mass, muscle size and maximal strength, similar to the normal-schedule group. Testosterone and IGF-1 fell in the TRE group. Overall, intermittent fasting appears to be a workable tool for controlling calorie intake, but there is no strong evidence that it builds more muscle than a normal meal pattern with the same intake.

Pre-sleep protein
Consuming about 30-40 g of casein protein before sleep can increase overnight muscle protein synthesis and metabolic rate without blunting fat breakdown (lipolysis). Casein digests slowly and keeps blood amino acids elevated for several hours, making it well suited to the long overnight fast. Cottage cheese, Greek yogurt or a casein shake are typical options.

Practical summary
- Hit daily calorie and protein totals first.
- Place a protein-rich meal in the few hours before and after training.
- Spread protein over 3-6 meals.
- Use fasting windows only if they suit appetite and lifestyle.
