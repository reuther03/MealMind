Sleep and Recovery
How much sleep adults and athletes need, what sleep loss does to appetite, strength, muscle building and fat loss, and practical ways to sleep better, including naps and evening nutrition.

Recommended sleep duration
- Adults: 7-9 hours of quality sleep per night.
- Adolescents: 8-10 hours per night. Adolescent athletes average only about 6.3 hours.
- Sleep research has led to the recommendation that adults get about 8 hours per night to avoid declines in attention and reaction time.
- Athletes often need the upper end of the range because of training load, travel and early sessions.

Sleep loss and appetite hormones
- Even one or two nights of short sleep raise ghrelin (the hunger hormone) and lower leptin (the fullness hormone).
- Sleep loss increases activity in brain reward areas and reduces prefrontal impulse control, so high-calorie foods feel more tempting even without real hunger.
- Short sleep is linked to higher overall energy intake and weight gain.
- A single night of sleep loss can cut glucose disposal by about 25%; one week of restricted sleep reduced insulin sensitivity by 11-20%.
- Two nights of restricted sleep raised total cortisol output by about 21%, and sleep-restricted people gained more abdominal and visceral fat in a controlled trial.

Sleep loss and muscle protein synthesis
- One night of total sleep deprivation reduced muscle protein synthesis by 18% the next day in healthy young men and women.
- In the same study, cortisol rose by 21% and testosterone fell by 24%, creating a catabolic hormonal environment.
- Chronic short sleep is therefore a risk for losing muscle mass and recovering poorly from training.

Sleep loss and strength and performance
- Effects depend on the task: submaximal, prolonged efforts (endurance, repeated sets) decline more than single maximal efforts.
- In one study, 24 hours without sleep did not change weightlifting performance, while 30 hours reduced sprint performance in team-sport athletes and 24 hours reduced endurance running performance.
- Swimmers who lost 2.5 hours of sleep per night for 4 nights kept grip and back strength but lost submaximal performance.
- Accuracy, reaction time and decision-making suffer early, which matters for skill sports and heavy lifting technique.

Sleep and fat loss while dieting
- When dieting, short sleep shifts weight loss toward more muscle and less fat, because hunger rises, cortisol rises and muscle protein synthesis falls.
- Protecting 7-9 hours of sleep is one of the simplest ways to preserve lean mass during a calorie deficit.

Sleep extension and naps
- Basketball players who extended their sleep for several weeks ran faster sprints and improved free-throw accuracy.
- After a night of only 4 hours sleep, a 30-minute lunchtime nap improved 20 m sprint performance and alertness.
- Naps are useful for athletes with early training, poor night sleep or afternoon sessions; a nap of about 30 minutes, taken early enough not to disturb night sleep, is a practical choice. Naps also lower salivary cortisol and help restore immune markers after a short night.

Sleep hygiene basics
- Keep a consistent bed and wake time, including weekends.
- Make the bedroom dark, quiet and cool, and keep screens out of bed.
- Limit caffeine late in the day and avoid alcohol close to bedtime, since both disrupt sleep quality.
- Build a relaxing wind-down routine before bed and manage evening stress; avoid very hard training late at night when possible.

Nutrition that affects sleep
- A high-glycemic carbohydrate meal eaten 4 hours before bed shortened the time to fall asleep more than the same meal eaten 1 hour before bed.
- Very large or very late meals can disturb sleep; a light evening snack is usually better.
- Tryptophan-rich foods help the body make serotonin and melatonin; a tryptophan-enriched cereal improved sleep efficiency in older adults.
- Tart cherry juice (about 30 ml of concentrate) contains melatonin and was linked to better sleep in small studies.
- Eating two kiwifruits one hour before bed for four weeks improved sleep quality scores in elite athletes.
