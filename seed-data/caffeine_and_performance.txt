Caffeine and Performance
Caffeine is a well-proven ergogenic aid; this guide covers the effective dose in mg/kg, when to take it before exercise, tolerance, safe daily limits for adults, genetic differences (CYP1A2, ADORA2A), side effects and effects on sleep.

What caffeine improves
Caffeine produces small to moderate improvements in muscular endurance, movement speed, strength, sprinting, jumping and throwing, plus many aerobic and anaerobic sport-specific tasks. Aerobic endurance shows the most consistent moderate-to-large benefit. Caffeine also improves attention, vigilance and reaction time, and can help both physical and mental performance after sleep loss. Trained and untrained people appear to benefit similarly.

How it works
Caffeine blocks adenosine receptors in the brain. Adenosine builds up during wakefulness and effort and promotes tiredness, so blocking it reduces perceived effort and fatigue and increases alertness and motivation.

Effective dose
- Consistent performance benefits are seen with 3-6 mg of caffeine per kg of body weight. For a 70 kg person that is about 210-420 mg.
- The minimum effective dose may be as low as 2 mg/kg.
- Very high doses (around 9 mg/kg) cause many side effects and are not needed for an ergogenic effect.
- For endurance exercise in the heat, 3-6 mg/kg is supported; at altitude, 4-6 mg/kg.

Timing before exercise
- The most common timing is about 60 minutes before exercise. Peak blood levels are reached roughly 30-120 minutes after an oral dose.
- Caffeine chewing gum is absorbed faster through the mouth and works best taken shortly before exercise.
- Because caffeine helps most when fatigue builds, taking some during the later stages of long events can work as well as a larger pre-exercise dose; low doses late in a race (for example flat cola) performed similarly to about 5-6 mg/kg taken an hour before.
- Coffee, capsules, gels, gum, bars and pre-workout drinks all work; capsules give the most predictable dose.

Tolerance and habituation
Habitual caffeine use does not consistently remove the performance benefit, and studies are mixed. In one study, low caffeine users who took caffeine daily for 4 weeks became tolerant and lost the acute ergogenic effect, while another study found the benefit remained after 20 days. Caffeine content of coffee varies 8- to 9-fold between similar shops, so self-reported intake is unreliable. Some athletes reduce caffeine for several days before key competitions, but this is not proven to be necessary.

Safe daily intake for adults
- The US FDA cites 400 mg per day, about two to three 12-ounce cups of coffee, as an amount not generally associated with negative effects for most healthy adults.
- Toxic effects such as seizures can occur with rapid intake of around 1200 mg, which is less than half a teaspoon of pure caffeine powder. Pure powders are therefore dangerous to measure at home.
- Energy drinks contain about 41-246 mg per 12 ounces. People who are pregnant, breastfeeding, sensitive or taking certain medications should talk to a health professional; energy drinks are not advised for children and teens.
- Caffeine is no longer banned in sport but is monitored by WADA; the urine limit of 12 micrograms per ml corresponds to about 10 mg/kg taken over several hours.

Genetics
Over 95% of caffeine is broken down in the liver by the CYP1A2 enzyme into paraxanthine, theobromine and theophylline. A common variant of the CYP1A2 gene (rs762551) divides people into fast (AA genotype) and slow (AC or CC) metabolizers. In the largest study, a 4 mg/kg dose improved 10 km cycling time by about 3% overall, but by 6.8% in fast metabolizers, while slow metabolizers did not improve. Other studies found different results, so genotype is not yet a reliable basis for dosing. Variants of the ADORA2A gene affect how much anxiety and sleep disruption caffeine causes.

Side effects
Common side effects include fast heart rate and palpitations, anxiety and jitters, headaches, stomach upset, insomnia and poorer sleep quality. Anxiety-prone athletes may perform worse if caffeine heightens pre-competition nerves.

Caffeine and sleep
Caffeine lengthens the time to fall asleep, reduces deep sleep, lowers sleep efficiency and shortens total sleep. Its half-life is usually 4-6 hours but varies widely. In one study, morning caffeine disturbed sleep in only 1 of 13 people, but a dose at 6 p.m. caused insomnia in 6. Evening caffeine before a night game delayed sleep onset and shortened sleep. To protect recovery, use caffeine earlier in the day and avoid it in the hours before bed.
