Dietary Fats and Omega-3
How much fat to eat, limits for saturated and trans fat, omega-3 (EPA, DHA, ALA) intakes, doses and food sources, and how very low-fat diets can affect hormones.

Why fat is essential
Fat supplies energy, helps absorb fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K), builds cell membranes and is needed to make hormones. Two fatty acids, linoleic acid (omega-6) and alpha-linolenic acid (ALA, omega-3), cannot be made by the body and must come from food.

Total fat intake
- WHO advises that adults get at least 15% of daily energy from fat and keep total fat at about 30% of energy or less to help prevent unhealthy weight gain.
- Sports nutrition reviews describe a normal athlete diet as 25-35% of energy from fat, about 0.5-1.5 g per kg of body weight per day (g/kg/day). Around 30% of calories is a typical recommendation.
- During very high-volume training, fat can reach up to 50% of calories to help meet large energy needs.
- When reducing body fat, intakes of 0.5-1 g/kg/day have been recommended, which may be as low as about 20% of calories.
- For physique athletes in contest preparation, 15-30% of calories from fat is suggested.
- 0.5 g/kg/day is a practical floor; going lower for long periods risks low essential fatty acid intake and hormonal side effects.

Saturated and trans fat limits
- WHO recommends no more than 10% of total energy from saturated fat and no more than 1% from trans fat of any kind. Lower intakes may bring extra health benefits.
- Saturated fat is found in fatty meat, butter, cheese, cream, ghee, lard, palm oil and coconut oil.
- Industrial trans fat is found in some baked and fried foods and pre-packaged snacks; ruminant trans fat occurs in meat and dairy.
- Replace saturated and trans fat with unsaturated fats from fish, avocado, nuts, seeds and olive, canola, soybean or sunflower oil.

Cholesterol and blood lipids
Blood cholesterol is influenced more by the type of fat in the diet than by dietary cholesterol alone. Diets high in saturated and trans fat raise LDL ("bad") cholesterol, while replacing them with polyunsaturated and monounsaturated fats improves the blood lipid profile. Long-chain omega-3s also lower blood triglycerides.

Omega-3 fatty acids
- ALA comes from plants: flaxseed oil (about 7.3 g per tablespoon), chia seeds (about 5.1 g per ounce), walnuts (about 2.6 g per ounce) and canola and soybean oils.
- The Adequate Intake (AI) for ALA is 1.6 g/day for men and 1.1 g/day for women.
- EPA and DHA are the long-chain forms found in fatty fish. A 3-ounce (85 g) cooked portion provides roughly: farmed Atlantic salmon 1.24 g DHA and 0.59 g EPA; Atlantic herring 0.94 g DHA and 0.77 g EPA; sardines 0.74 g DHA and 0.45 g EPA; Atlantic mackerel 0.59 g DHA and 0.43 g EPA.
- The body converts ALA to EPA and DHA very poorly, usually less than 15%, so plant sources do not replace fish.
- Heart health advisories recommend 1-2 servings of seafood per week.

Omega-3 supplements
- A typical 1000 mg fish oil capsule contains about 180 mg EPA and 120 mg DHA, but products vary widely; read the label for EPA plus DHA content, not total oil.
- Algal oil is a vegan option that usually provides 100-300 mg DHA per serving.
- Forms include natural triglycerides, ethyl esters and re-esterified triglycerides; krill oil contains phospholipids.
- Safety: EFSA concluded that combined EPA and DHA intakes up to about 5 g/day are safe long term, and the US FDA considers supplements providing no more than 5 g/day safe. Very high intakes (2-15 g/day) may increase bleeding time, so people taking anticoagulants should consult a doctor.

Low-fat diets and hormones
Higher-fat diets appear to maintain circulating testosterone better than low-fat diets, and men in studies with very low fat intake have shown lower testosterone. Separating the effect of fat from total calories and from the type of fat is difficult, but extremely low fat intake is not recommended for people who train. Very high-fat or ketogenic diets have not shown performance benefits and often cause stomach problems during exercise.
