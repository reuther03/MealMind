Alcohol and Fitness
How alcohol affects muscle protein synthesis, recovery, hydration, hormones and body composition, what counts as a standard drink, and how to drink with the least harm to training.

Calories in alcohol
- Pure alcohol provides about 29 kJ per gram, roughly 7 kcal per gram, almost as much as fat and nearly twice as much as carbohydrate or protein.
- Alcohol calories come with no useful nutrients, and drinking often leads to extra snacking and poorer food choices.
- The body prioritizes clearing alcohol, and heavy intake can impair liver glucose output and glycogen storage.

Standard drink definitions
- In the United States, one standard drink contains about 14 g (0.6 fl oz) of pure alcohol.
- One standard drink equals 12 fl oz (355 ml) of regular beer at 5% alcohol, 5 fl oz (148 ml) of wine at 12%, or 1.5 fl oz (44 ml) of 40% spirits.
- 8-10 fl oz of malt liquor or hard seltzer at about 7% is also one drink.
- A 12 oz craft beer at 10% alcohol counts as two standard drinks; light beers still contain about 85% of the alcohol of regular beer.
- Definitions differ between countries, so check local guidance when comparing limits.

Alcohol and muscle protein synthesis
- In a key study (Parr et al., 2014), 8 active men did resistance exercise (8 sets of 5 reps at 80% of 1RM) plus cycling, then drank alcohol at 1.5 g per kg of body weight, about 12 standard drinks.
- Compared with protein alone, alcohol plus protein reduced myofibrillar protein synthesis by 24%, and alcohol plus carbohydrate reduced it by 37% over 8 hours of recovery.
- Alcohol suppressed activation of mTOR, the main signaling pathway that switches on muscle building.
- Eating protein partly protected muscle, but did not fully cancel the effect.
- A systematic review of 12 studies on drinking after resistance training found lower testosterone, higher cortisol and lower muscle protein synthesis, which suggests long-term muscle gains could suffer if post-workout drinking is regular.

Alcohol and recovery
- In one study, drinking 1 g of alcohol per kg of body weight right after hard eccentric exercise made the loss of isometric, concentric and eccentric strength worse 36 hours later.
- Most studies found little change in creatine kinase, soreness or next-day force at moderate doses, so the main concern is impaired adaptation rather than immediate weakness.
- Alcohol after prolonged cycling modestly slowed glycogen resynthesis, largely because it replaced carbohydrate in meals; over 24 hours glycogen stores recovered.
- Alcohol disrupts sleep length and quality, which slows recovery further.

Alcohol and hydration
- Alcohol suppresses antidiuretic hormone; early research found about 10 ml of extra urine for each gram of alcohol.
- The effect depends on strength: drinks above about 4% alcohol clearly increase urine output, drinks above 2% reduced fluid retention during rehydration in one study, and drinks up to 4% did not raise urine output in well-hydrated people in another. When dehydrated, the body's drive to retain fluid partly overrides the diuretic effect.
- After exercise, rehydrate with water, electrolytes and food before any alcohol, and avoid strong drinks while still fluid depleted.

Alcohol and hormones
- Free testosterone was lower 12-24 hours after a high alcohol dose following resistance exercise in one trial, while other trials saw no clear change at lower doses.
- Cortisol tends to rise with alcohol, shifting the body toward a catabolic state.

Alcohol and performance
- Alcohol impairs balance, reaction time, coordination and fine motor skills in a dose-dependent way.
- Endurance performance drops once blood alcohol is meaningfully elevated, and drinking before training or competition is not advised.
- Binge drinking after games is common in team sports, and it compounds harm by displacing food, fluid and sleep.

Practical limits for people who train
- US dietary guidance suggests no more than 2 drinks per day for men and no more than 1 for women, and less is better for health.
- Avoid binge sessions, especially after key workouts or during muscle-gain or fat-loss phases.
- If you drink, eat a protein-rich meal and rehydrate after training first, choose lower-alcohol drinks, alternate with water and count the calories in your daily total.
- Keep heavy drinking away from the 24 hours after hard training, when muscle repair is most active.
